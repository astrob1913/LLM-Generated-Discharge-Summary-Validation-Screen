Using the validation screen

What this screen shows, what every part of it means, and what it does not do

Synthetic record — no real patient

The screen sets a discharge summary written by a language model beside all thirty ward notes it was written from, and asks a reviewer to decide, line by line, whether the record supports each statement. This page explains it. Nothing here needs to be read before opening the screen — it is written so that any part can be looked up afterwards.

1. Why the screen exists

When a patient leaves hospital, a discharge summary goes to their GP. It is often the only account the GP receives of what happened. A language model can now draft that letter from the patient’s notes, but a clinician still has to check it and sign it — and the clinician checking it usually did not look after that patient.

Checking the letter on its own is not enough, for one reason above all others. An omission leaves no trace. If the model drops something, there is nothing in the letter to notice. A reviewer can read it from top to bottom, find every sentence properly supported, and still have missed notes the model never used. The only way to catch that is to account for the record as well as the document.

So the screen does two things at once: it walks through every statement in the summary, and it accounts for every note in the record. It offers no score, no percentage and no ranking, and it never tells the reviewer which problem matters most.

2. Before you start

It needs a laptop or desktop

The screen refuses a browser window narrower than 820 pixels or shorter than 560. It is a two-column comparison with a five-day chart and a thirty-record grid, and a phone cannot show them together. If the refusal notice appears on a computer, widening the window clears it. There is a link at the foot of that notice for anyone who wants to look regardless.

Nothing you type leaves your browser

Ticks, flags and typed reasons are kept on your own device. They are not sent anywhere, and nobody else can see them.

You can stop and come back

The review is saved as you go and restored when the page is opened again, with a line at the top saying how many checks were already made and when they were last saved. A thirty-four statement document does not have to be finished in one sitting.

The patient is invented

Every name, identifier, note and date on the screen comes from a synthetic dataset. No real person, record or NHS number appears anywhere.

3. The five steps

The same five steps are inside the screen, behind the How do I use this screen? button in the key bar.

  1. Read one line of the summary and click it. The evidence panel then shows only the records that bear on that line, and nothing else. On a wide screen the evidence sits beside the summary; on a narrower one it sits below it.
  2. Look at the evidence. Click any record in the list to see the exact row it came from, word for word, with the file name and row number. You get the whole note, not an extract, so the box scrolls — and it opens at the line the summary drew on. Nothing is paraphrased and nothing is cut out, including the typing errors in the original.
  3. Say what you think. Press ✓ if the line is fine, or ! if something is wrong with it, then type one line saying what. Every problem appears on a list at the bottom of the screen, with the record that proves it, ready to copy and send back.
  4. The researcher’s reading is folded away. Wherever a dashed Show the researcher’s reading button appears, there is a sentence written by hand for this demonstration saying what the researcher makes of that line. Nothing on the screen computed it. It is folded so a reviewer can form their own view first. Open them one at a time, or switch them all on with the button in the key bar.
  5. Deal with the leftovers in the strip at the bottom. Those are notes from the admission that no line of the summary uses. Either they did not need to be in the summary (press Accept) or something was left out (press Read all).

4. Every part of the screen

Described in the order you meet them, from the top of the page down.

The frame at the top

Patient banner, the two counters, the Approve and send button, the colour key, and a one-line instruction that changes as you work. It stays on screen so the state of the review is never out of view. On a narrow window the key collapses behind a Key button and opens when pressed.

Overview — every section against all thirty records

A grid: each section of the summary down the side, the thirty records across the top in the order they were written. Each small square is one record, coloured by what that record does to that section. A wider gap between columns marks the start of a new day. This is for locating risk before reading anything — a row with red squares late in the admission is worth opening first. Clicking a row jumps to that section.

Against the PRSB standard

Folded away until opened. The PRSB eDischarge Summary Standard lists twenty-two sections at three conformance levels — six mandatory, ten required, six optional. The panel prints all twenty-two and marks each present or absent in this summary. A section the model never wrote has no line to check, so it would otherwise be invisible; this is where it shows. An absent mandatory or required section carries the same pair of marks as a statement and must be answered for. Absent optional sections are shown but not gated, because leaving one out breaks no rule.

The generated summary, on the left

The letter exactly as the model wrote it. Every line is a control. Each carries the two marks, a coloured dot showing what the record does to it, and the conformance level of its section. Marking a line as a problem opens a one-line box for the reason and lets a record be attached as proof.

Evidence in time, on the right

When a statement is selected, the records that bear on it are placed on the admission’s time axis in three rows — emergency, ward and medical, therapies — because where a piece of evidence sits is itself informative. A rule at the bottom shows the stretch of time the evidence covers. A dot off the axis on the left means no record bears on the statement at all. Clicking any dot opens that record.

The record cards, beneath

The ward notes themselves, in full and exactly as written, with the file name and row number. Inside each card the clause the summary took is marked in blue, and a clause that no statement in the summary carries is underlined in amber. A clause is never both.

Most notes are longer than the box, so the card scrolls and opens at the highlighted clause rather than at the top. A soft fade at the top or bottom edge means there is more text in that direction.

The dose panel, for a medication

A medication is not a single fact but a value that moves through the admission, so selecting a drug statement opens a second chart: every record naming that drug placed in time, with the interval the summary reports shaded. Chips beneath list every change in order. The sentence under the chart is generated by counting the points, not written by hand.

Problems found

Everything flagged, each with its typed reason and the record attached as proof, and a copy control. This is what leaves the screen.

The two accounting strips, at the foot

Records not used by any statement. Notes from the admission that no line of the summary draws on, grouped so a whole group can be accepted in one decision, or read in full first.

Lines inside used records that no statement covers. A record counts as used the moment one statement draws on it — but a note can support one line of the summary and still contain an instruction that reaches the letter nowhere. Those passages are listed separately, quoted from the source row, and decided one at a time. Without this strip a record could be counted as fully accounted for while something important inside it had been read by nobody.

The two you press

Pressing the same mark again clears it. Setting a mark also selects the line, so the evidence is on screen at the moment of judging.

The dot beside each line — what the record does to it

the record backs it up
the record says more than the summary does
the record disagrees, or nothing supports it
the record cannot settle it

Inside a record card

blue
the clause the summary took from this note
amber
a clause in this note that no statement carries

These two never overlap. A clause is either one or the other, never both, and that is true by the way the screen is built rather than by anyone checking it each time.

6. The two counters

The Approve and send button stays locked until both are full. That is the whole guarantee the screen offers, so it is worth understanding exactly what each one counts.

CounterWhat it counts
Checks decided Every decision the review requires: each statement in the summary, each absent mandatory or required PRSB section, and each line inside a used record that nothing carries. All three kinds must be answered.
Records accounted for How many of the thirty ward notes have been dealt with. A note counts either because at least one statement draws on it, or because the reviewer has explicitly accepted a group of unused notes as having nothing that needed to reach the summary.
Problems found How many lines have been flagged so far. Not a target — simply a running count.

The second counter does not start at zero, and that is not a bug. It opens part-full because the summary already draws on most of the record. What is left is the handful of notes no statement uses — and those are the ones a reviewer would otherwise never see, which is exactly why the counter exists. Read it as “this many of the thirty have been dealt with”, not as “this much of my work is done”.

7. What the screen works out, and what was written by hand

This distinction matters more than anything else on this page, and the screen states it in its own footer too.

Worked out by the screenWritten by hand for this demonstration
The counts and both counters. Whether each PRSB section is present or absent, and which absences must be answered for. The sentence under the dose chart. The placing of records on the time axis. Which checks remain. Which records bear on which statement. Which clause inside a record the summary took. The list of lines that nothing carries. Every sentence behind a Show the researcher’s reading button, and every sentence describing an absent section.

So when the screen says “nothing found” for an absent section, that is a person having read all thirty records, not a search result. The links between statement and record were matched by hand for this one episode, and every one of them can be read back from the source row printed on the card.

8. What the screen does not do

  • It does not compute its own links. The candidate records shown for each statement were matched by hand for this episode. A deployed version would propose ranked candidates that the reviewer can overrule; this one demonstrates the mechanism, not the extraction.
  • It does not rank anything. There is no score, no percentage, no confidence rating and no ordering by severity. Statements appear in the order the model wrote them. An interface that points at three problems has implied the rest are fine, and this one refuses to make that claim.
  • It guarantees attention, not correctness. A reviewer can mark every check without reading any of them. Nothing prevents that. What the screen guarantees is that nothing was passed over without a decision being recorded.
  • It covers one admission. Everything on the page belongs to a single synthetic episode of thirty notes over five days.
  • It is not a medical device. It is a research prototype built for an MSc Data Science dissertation and is not for clinical use.
