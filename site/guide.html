Companion notes

Using the validation screen

This page explains what the screen is, what it asks you to do, and what every part of it means. You do not have to read it first — the screen has its own How do I use this screen? button with the short version. This is here for anyone who wants the fuller picture, or who would rather know what they are looking at before they open it.

Needs a laptop, desktop or tablet Allow about half an hour Nothing you type leaves your browser The patient is invented

What the screen is for

When a patient leaves hospital, someone writes a discharge summary: the letter that tells the GP what happened. Large language models can now draft that letter from the ward notes in seconds. The difficulty is not writing it — it is checking it. To know whether a generated summary is safe to send, a clinician has to hold it against the record it came from, and reading thirty ward notes takes about as long as writing the letter by hand. So in practice the summary gets skimmed and signed.

This screen is an attempt at making that check practical. It puts every line of a generated summary next to the exact rows of the record that bear on it, and it asks the reviewer to say something about every line before the summary can go out. It is a research prototype built for an MSc dissertation, not a product.

The case on screen

One admission, five days on a neurology ward, thirty notes. The summary was generated from those notes by a language model and is shown word for word as it was produced — nothing has been tidied up, and no mistake has been added. It has twelve sections and thirty-four statements.

The patient does not exist. The record is synthetic, generated for this project, and the name, NHS number and date of birth are invented.

What you are being asked to do

Fifty decisions, of three different kinds.

The counter at the top left reads Checks decided — 0 of 50. Those fifty are not all the same kind of thing, and it helps to know the three kinds before you start:

  • 34 statements that the summary actually wrote. For each one: does the record support it?
  • 7 sections that the national standard for discharge summaries expects, which this summary never wrote at all. For each one: does it matter that it is missing?
  • 9 lines sitting inside records that the summary did draw on, which nothing in the summary carries. For each one: should it have been included?

A second counter, Records accounted for — 24 of 30, counts something different. It is not decisions; it is coverage of the record. Twenty-four of the thirty notes are already used by at least one statement. The remaining six are in a strip at the foot of the page and have to be dealt with by hand.

Fifty is a lot, and that is one of the open questions

Whether a clinician would tolerate fifty checks on a real document is not known. It has never been measured, here or anywhere the author could find. The argument for checking everything is that in a discharge summary any line can carry harm; the argument against is that people stop reading alerts they see too many of. If it feels like too much, that is a finding, and saying so is more useful than finishing.

Before you open it

It will not open on a phone

A phone shows a short card asking you to open the link on a computer instead. This is deliberate. The screen's whole idea is that a claim and its evidence should be in front of you at the same moment, and a phone cannot show two columns of text, a five-day chart and a thirty-record grid together. Any browser window at least 820 pixels wide and 560 tall will work — a laptop, a desktop, or a tablet held upright. If you are on a computer and still see the card, the browser window is simply too narrow; widening it is enough.

There is a small link at the foot of that card to open it anyway, if you would rather look than wait. It will be cramped.

Nothing you do is sent anywhere

There is no account, no login and no server behind this page. Every tick, flag and typed note is kept in your own browser, on your own machine, and nobody else can see it — not the author, not anyone. You can close the tab and come back later and it will still be there. There is no tracking of any kind on the page.

The consequence is worth knowing in advance: your notes do not reach anyone unless you send them. How to do that is in the last section.

The five steps

The same five that the How do I use this screen? button shows, with a little more around them.

1

Read one line of the summary and click it

The summary sits on the left of a wide window, above the evidence on a narrower one. Every statement in it is a button. Click one and the rest of the screen changes to show only what bears on that line, and nothing else.

2

Look at the evidence

Click any record to see the exact row it came from, word for word, with the file name and the row number. Nothing on this screen is paraphrased or shortened. If you cannot trust that what you are reading is the record, the screen is worth nothing, so the full text is always there.

3

Say what you think

Press ✓ It's fine if the line is supported, or ! Something's wrong if it is not — and then type one line saying what is wrong with it.

The typed line is the most important thing on the screen. A flag with no reason cannot be acted on by whoever has to fix the summary. Everything you flag collects into a list at the bottom of the page, with the record that proves it.

4

Deal with the leftovers

Two strips at the foot of the page hold what the main pass does not reach. The first is whole records that no statement uses at all; they are grouped, so you can accept a group in one decision or open it and read every record in it. The second is lines inside records the summary did use, which nothing in the summary carries — a record stops looking suspicious the moment one statement draws on it, and that strip exists because of it.

5

Finish

The Approve and send button at the top right stays inactive until all fifty checks are decided and every record accounted for. If anything is flagged, it changes to Send back — N problems. The screen will not let you approve a summary you have just said is wrong.

What each part of the screen shows

Top to bottom, in the order you meet them.

The bar at the very top

Who the patient is, the three counters, and the approve button. It stays pinned to the top of the window so that at any point in the task you can see how much is left. The approve button sits next to the counters on purpose — the way out is beside the count of what stands between you and it.

The key, and "Do this next"

Two more pinned strips. The key names every colour on the screen, and it is pinned rather than printed once at the top because a key that scrolls away while its marks are still on screen is not a key. On a narrower window it folds behind a Key button to save room.

The blue strip below it reads the state of your review and names the single next thing to do. It changes as you work.

Overview — every section against all thirty records

One row per section of the summary, in document order. Thirty squares across, one per record, in time order, with a wider gap where a new day starts. It is not meant to be read closely; it is meant to show you where the trouble is concentrated before you start going line by line. Click any row to jump to that section. It folds away once it has done its job.

Against the PRSB standard

This panel is closed until you open it. It lists all twenty-two sections of the PRSB eDischarge Summary Standard — the national specification for what a discharge summary should contain — and marks which of them this summary wrote and which it did not.

It exists because of a gap in everything above it. A wrong line is visible: it is on the screen and you can argue with it. A section that was never written has nothing to click on and nothing to be suspicious of. Printing the whole standard is the only way it becomes visible at all.

One caution about the labels. Mandatory, required and optional describe what a system must be able to transmit, not how clinically important a section is. An optional section can matter more to a patient than a mandatory one. The screen requires an answer for the mandatory and required absences and lets the optional ones pass, which is a rule about the standard, not about clinical risk.

Evidence in time

A five-day axis with three rows, because where a note was written matters: emergency, ward and medical, therapies. Each dot is one record. The bar underneath shows the stretch of time the evidence for the selected line covers. If a statement has no evidence at all, its marker sits off the axis on the left with a dashed red outline, so that "nothing supports this" is a visible position rather than an empty chart. Click any dot to read its source row.

The dose chart

Appears only when the selected statement names a drug. It plots the dose at every record that mentions that drug across the admission, with a shaded band showing what the summary reports. The sentence underneath it is counted straight off the chart.

The record cards

The records themselves, in full, with note type, timestamp and row number. Two markings appear inside the text: a blue highlight means the summary took that clause, an amber underline means nothing in the summary carries it. No clause can be both.

Problems found

Green while it is empty, red as soon as anything is in it. Every flag you raise appears here with the line you typed, the statement it concerns and the record you cited. This is what the screen exists to produce.

The colours

The same key that is pinned at the top of the screen, written out.

✓ This line is fine. The record supports it as written.
! Something is wrong with it. Typing one line of reason is part of this decision, not an extra.
Blue — the record backs it up.
Amber — the record says more than the summary does. Usually the interesting one: the summary is not wrong, it is incomplete.
Red — the record disagrees, or nothing supports it.
Grey — the record cannot settle it. Some statements are about care after discharge, and no note from this admission can prove or disprove them. Forcing a verdict on those would be asking you to guess.
In the overview grid, an empty square means that record does not bear on that section at all.

What the screen worked out, and what a person wrote

The most important thing to understand about this prototype.

Some of what you see was computed from the data. Some of it was written by hand. Those two are not interchangeable, and the screen labels the boundary rather than letting you assume.

Computed by the screen

The counts and the progress. Which standard sections are present and which are absent, and which absences require an answer. The sentence under the dose chart that begins with a figure. The running "Do this next" instruction. Whether the approve button is available.

Matched by hand

Which records bear on which statement was decided by a person, not by software. Automatic matching was attempted and it did not work well enough to use: the best of three methods scored 0.56 on a combined measure of how many of the links it proposed were right and how many of the real links it found — 23 correct, 20 spurious, 16 missed. A tool that is right about half the time and does not say which half is not something a clinician can rely on, so the links here are hand-made and declared as such in the footer of the screen.

The same applies to the clauses highlighted and underlined inside each record.

Written by hand, and folded away

Wherever you see a dashed Show the researcher's reading button, there is a sentence behind it giving the author's own view of that line. Nothing on the screen produced it.

It is folded shut on purpose. If you read someone else's conclusion before forming your own, you end up agreeing with a finding rather than making one, and for a study whose whole point is what a clinician notices, that would spoil the answer. Open them whenever you like — there is also one switch in the key bar that opens all of them at once — but the suggestion is to decide first and look afterwards. Comparing the two is often the useful part.

What this prototype does not do

Stated plainly, so nobody has to discover them by being caught out.

  • It is one patient. One admission, thirty notes. Nothing here shows how often any of these problems occur in general, and it is not designed to.
  • It does not scale as it stands. A long admission with two hundred notes would overflow the overview grid and turn fifty checks into several hundred. At that size the design would have to triage rather than list everything.
  • It does not score or rank anything. There is no confidence figure and no ordering of candidates. Every record shown against a statement was put there by hand.
  • Nothing stops a reviewer ticking everything. The screen makes a tick a recorded act rather than a default, but flagging costs more effort than ticking, which is arguably the wrong way round.
  • Nobody has used it before. The estimate of half an hour is an estimate. Whether the screen is faster than simply reading the record has not been measured.
Which is why criticism of the screen is as useful as anything found in the summary

If something is confusing, in the wrong place, or asks for work that is not worth doing, that is a result. It does not need to be phrased carefully.

Sending your notes back

The one step the screen cannot do for you.

Because there is no server, your review stays on your own machine. To pass it on:

  1. Scroll to the Problems found panel near the foot of the page.
  2. Press Copy the list. Everything you flagged, with your reasons and the records cited, goes to your clipboard as plain text.
  3. Paste it into a reply to whoever sent you the link.
Without that step, nothing reaches anyone

There is no automatic collection of any kind. If you close the tab without copying the list, your notes stay on your machine and the work is lost to everyone else. The review itself is saved, so you can come back and copy it later — but only from the same browser on the same device.

Anything that does not fit in that list — a thought about the layout, a question about why something is where it is, a judgement about whether one of the findings actually matters clinically — is best written in your own words in the reply. It will not be captured by the screen.
